## 4. Empirical Claims, Facts & Numerics Audit

Every factual claim, date, proper noun, and numeric reference was cross-checked against `safe_sources.json`:

### 4.1 Numerical & Historical Verification
- **1915 Coca-Cola Brief (Slide 6):** The design brief issued by the Coca-Cola Bottling Association challenged glass manufacturers to design a bottle recognized by feel in the dark or broken on the ground. Fully supported by **S4**.
- **November 16, 1915 Patent (Slide 7 & 8):** U.S. Design Patent D48,160 granted to Alexander Samuelson of the Root Glass Company based on an elongated cocoa bean illustration. Fully supported by **S4**.
- **19th Century Tiffany Hue (Slide 11):** Charles Lewis Tiffany selected robin's-egg blue for the cover of Tiffany's Blue Book. Fully supported by **S5**.
- **1906 New York Sun Report (Slide 12):** Ironclad rule that a Tiffany Blue Box cannot be bought or taken from the building without a purchased store item. Fully supported by **S5**.
- **1998 Trademark & 2001 Pantone 1837 Blue (Slide 13):** Tiffany registered the color trademark in 1998 and standardized custom private color "1837 Blue" with Pantone in 2001 (named for founding year 1837). Trademark scope is properly restricted to jewelry boxes, retail packaging, and related goods. Fully supported by **S5**.
- **1984 IKEA Visual Profile Manual (Slide 14):** First formal manual established trademark and wordmark rules; red-and-white logos persisted in Scandinavia for years. Fully supported by **S6**.
- **1991 IKEA Architectural Standardization (Slide 14):** Global transition to building exclusively blue warehouse stores with yellow wordmarks on the facade. Fully supported by **S6**.
- **1986 Shopping Observations & 1989 Hamburg Test (Slide 15):** Ingvar Kamprad and Sten Lundén observed purchase abandonment in 1986; yellow polypropylene bags tested in Hamburg in 1989 caused checkout confusion; operational dilemma solved by retaining yellow bags as in-store loaners and selling blue FRAKTA bags at checkout. Verified details: crinkled blue polypropylene with blue webbing straps featuring yellow lettering. Fully supported by **S7**.
- **Late 2009 McDonald's European Green Rollout (Slide 10):** Swapping traditional red backdrops for deep hunter green across Europe while keeping Golden Arches invariant. Fully supported by **S8**.
- **2018 McDonald's Canada "Follow the Arches" (Slide 10):** Cossette campaign (Feb 13, 2018) using cropped sections of the golden arches paired with directional copy ("on your right", "on your left"). Explicitly clarified as cropped logo fragments with text, not color-alone proof. Fully supported by **S9**.

### 4.2 Exclusion of Unsubstantiated Claims
- **Zero Invented Percentages:** No claims of "85% of shoppers recognize...", "70% brand lift", or other fabricated survey figures.
- **No Universal Color Psychology:** Slide 5 directly addresses and rejects the myth that colors hold fixed universal emotional meanings or biological triggers. It correctly grounds brand color equity in Ehrenberg-Bass research (**S1**): commercial value derives from consistent and exclusive market use over time, not innate preference for blue over yellow.
- **Clear Demarcation of Classroom Demonstrations:** Slides 2, 3, and 16 are explicitly presented as pedagogical classroom illustrations. The scripts acknowledge that recognition varies based on consumer shopping habits, familiarity, and geographic context, avoiding any claim of universal recognition.
