Thanks, Can. IKEA created another famous clue: the blue FRAKTA bag. In 1986, shoppers got tired carrying heavy goods. IKEA tested yellow bags in 1989, but checkout got confusing. So yellow bags stayed inside for borrowing. Blue bags were sold to take home. That durable bag became a familiar household clue.

SLIDE 16

What do these examples teach us about design? First, color alone is rarely enough. Many big companies use the same basic colors. Red is used by Coca-Cola, Target, and McDonald’s. Yellow is shared by IKEA, Shell, and DHL. Without context, one color can suggest several brands.

SLIDE 17

This is why visual clues work best together. A plain bottle outline could be any drink. A solid red square could mean many brands. But when you pair shape with color, recognition becomes easy. Not everyone will spot every clue instantly. Still, multiple clues support each other during shopping.

SLIDE 18

Our next lesson is about the risk of redesigns. Design teams often want to change packaging for new trends. But shoppers rely on quick visual habits in store aisles. If a company changes colors or shapes too fast, customers get confused. Protecting familiar clues keeps existing buyers happy.

SLIDE 19

Here are three practical tips for designers. First, build clear shapes that work in black and white. Second, pair colors with unique packages and fonts. Third, stay consistent over long periods of time. You can evolve cues carefully, but test before changing them. Consistency turns simple designs into lasting memory shortcuts.
